How to read every map. Each is drawn from the real authored network — the model, not a sketch.

━━ thick amber = fast arterial / thoroughfare  ·  ── thin steel = ordinary side street
──▶ arrowhead = a one-way street (two-way streets have no arrow)  ·  ── coral = a right-turn-only lane
● signal (a learning agent)  ·  ▪ all-way stop  ·  ▲ yield  ·  ▪ boundary (traffic enters/leaves)
▨ faint amber band = a heavy origin→destination demand flow

coord rating = how much a neighbour-aware policy should beat a blind one (▲ large · ● moderate). build = 🟢 live here · 🟡 needs care · 🔴 needs a schema/phase extension.

Turns are fully modelled: each lane carries a turn mask, movements are classified geometrically, the router only ever routes through legal turns, and phases derive from the surviving movements. "No left off the arterial" and "right-in/right-out only" are both just masks. The eight scenarios below are live sim levels (their maps come straight from --dump) — including the diagonal, which is live only because we grew the observation schema for it; the last two need further schema/phase work and are drawn as schematics.

1 · One-way couplet grid

▲ coord · 🟢 built · 25 signals

Every street is one-way and parallel streets run opposite directions — a Manhattan-style couplet on both axes. Left turns are banned everywhere (through + right only), the real rule that keeps one-ways flowing.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. The whole payoff is green-wave progression: phase offsets between adjacent signals so a platoon rides a wall of greens. That is the canonical thing a neighbour-aware shared policy can learn and a blind per-intersection policy structurally cannot. The cleanest coordination test.

2 · One-way pair + thoroughfares

▲ coord · 🟢 built · 20 signals

Two adjacent one-ways running opposite directions (the amber-glow couplet, rows 2–3) crossed by two-way thoroughfares (the vertical arterials). No left turns off the arterials.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. The signals must run two coupled rhythms at once — progress the couplet east/west and platoon-serve the heavy thoroughfare crossings. Independent agents can't phase-lock the couplet against the arterial surges. This is your exact ask and the best single headline scenario.

3 · Access-managed arterial (RIRO)

● coord · 🟢 built · 18 signals

A fast two-way arterial (amber) where every side street is right-in / right-out only — the coral links. A car on a side street can only turn right onto the arterial; it cannot cross or turn left.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. The turn ban reshapes routing, not just phasing: the turn-aware router sends blocked trips right and around the block automatically, concentrating load onto specific right-turn pockets the signals must learn to serve. A clean demo that banning turns changes where the traffic goes.

5 · Diverge / converge couplet

● coord · 🟢 built · 20 signals

A two-way arterial spine (centre column) flanked by a one-way pair — northbound on one side, southbound on the other — so traffic splits around the block and rejoins. Lefts banned on the one-ways.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. Merge / diverge metering: the join signals must not starve or overfill either leg. Tests coordination across an asymmetric topology rather than a regular grid — the split/rejoin points are where a learned policy earns its keep.

6 · Tidal one-way pair

● coord · 🟢 built · 20 signals

The same couplet as #2, but demand is time-varying: the eastbound leg peaks early and drains, the westbound leg does the opposite — a rush-hour tide reversing the dominant direction across the episode.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. Non-stationary and directional. The policy must shift green split toward whichever way is surging and shift it back later — something a fixed-time plan cannot track. Extends the earlier corridor-rush result with real structure.

7 · Platoon surge

● coord · 🟢 built · 25 signals

A one-way grid fed by a ramp boundary (lower-left) that releases sharp platoons — short high-rate pulses, not smooth Poisson arrivals (the amber band traces the platoon's path across the grid).

Why it benefits from the shared policy. Bursty, correlated arrivals. A signal must flush a platoon then recover, and the downstream signals must catch the platoon — progression again, but triggered by demand rather than baked into offsets. Tests robustness to non-Poisson load.

8 · Heterogeneous control

▲ coord · 🟢 built · 16 signals

A signalized grid with an arterial, but several minor crossings are not signals: all-way stops (coral squares) and a yield (green triangle). Only the signals are learning agents; the stops/yields run their own fixed rules.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. The observation already carries each neighbour's control type — this is the one scenario that exercises it. The shared policy can learn to lean on a stop-controlled neighbour differently than a signalized one; an independent agent is blind to that. Coordination across control types.

10 · Diagonal arterial (now live — schema v3)

▲ coord · 🟢 built · 24 signals

A one-way diagonal thoroughfare cutting NE across the grid, so where it passes through a junction it adds a fifth (SW) leg — a 5-leg intersection with an acute-angle crossing. Only right turns are allowed off the diagonal. Building this forced the observation schema to grow from 4 to 8 octant approach slots (v3, obs 105→233) and the diagonal nodes to a per-approach 5-phase plan; ordinary 4-leg nodes are unchanged.

Why it benefits from the shared policy. The diagonal is a tightly-coupled arterial chain (like the corridor), so the green wave should pay — but its 5-leg nodes are exactly where a 1-hop MLP view is weakest and where a coupling-aware attention/GNN policy earns its place. The scenario the whole ladder was pointing at.

Two that still push past today's schema

One high-conflict crossing becomes a chain of simple 2-phase ones — every U-turn signal must be offset-timed to the mainline platoon.
